香闺绣阁

xiāng guī xiù gé
Literal: "fragrant, boudoir, embroidered, chamber"
Home › Lifestyle › 香闺绣阁

📖 Meaning

In old China this idiom referred to a woman's private inner chamber — an elegant, ornate boudoir where young ladies of wealthy, respectable families lived a sheltered life. It carries connotations of refinement, seclusion, and the protective luxury of an upper-class upbringing, and is often used to contrast with poverty or hardship.

📜 Origin Story

The idiom first appears in Scene 39 of The Story of the Lute (《琵琶记》), a southern drama by the Yuan dynasty playwright Gao Ming. In that scene the heroine humbly says that noble ladies are 'beauties of fragrant boudoirs and embroidered chambers' (香闺绣阁之名姝), while she herself is merely a poor woman in hempen skirt and thorn hairpin (裙布荆钗之贫妇). The phrase thus entered the language to describe the luxurious inner chambers of high-born households, and was later picked up in the Qing dynasty novel Dream of the Red Chamber (《红楼梦》), where it also describes the ladies' quarters.

💡 Example Usage

她自幼养在香闺绣阁，从未尝过民间疾苦。

Having been raised in a fragrant boudoir since childhood, she had never tasted the hardships of common life.
